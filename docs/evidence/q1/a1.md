# Q1 — Use case design

## Context

**Industry:** Legal services — mid-market law firm  
**Company size:** 120 lawyers across 4 practice areas (corporate M&A, employment, IP, litigation); 40 paralegals and legal ops staff; total headcount ~200  
**Problem today:** Associates spend 30–40% of billable time on document retrieval, precedent research, and drafting first versions of contracts or memos. The firm uses a shared network drive with 15 years of case files, contract templates, and internal memos — completely unsearchable beyond filename. Two partners experimented with ChatGPT Enterprise for 90 days but abandoned it after a data breach scare at a peer firm. The core tension: legal work is confidentiality-critical by profession, yet the productivity gap vs AI-enabled competitors is now visible in pitch decks.

---

## Personas

### Persona 1 — Senior Associate (María, M&A)
María bills 2,200 hours/year. Her daily loop: receive a draft SPA from opposing counsel, compare it against the firm's standard positions, flag deviations, and draft a redline. Today she does this manually against a mental model of prior deals. Pain points: (a) she cannot efficiently search 8 years of executed SPAs for market-standard positions on specific clauses; (b) every search is a context switch that breaks her drafting flow; (c) sending documents to any external AI is a professional conduct risk she cannot take.

### Persona 2 — Legal Operations Analyst (Carlos, Legal Ops)
Carlos runs the firm's matter management system and owns the DMS (document management system). His workflow: onboard new matter types, maintain document templates, track utilisation metrics. Pain points: (a) he has no way to expose the firm's knowledge base to the lawyers without building a bespoke search tool; (b) every new AI initiative requires IT sign-off and security review, which creates a 6-week queue; (c) he cannot demo anything to partners without a live system they can poke.

### Persona 3 — Managing Partner (Elena, Governance)
Elena approves technology spend and answers to the firm's professional indemnity insurer. Her workflow: quarterly technology review, vendor due-diligence sign-off. Pain points: (a) vendor SaaS means client data leaves the jurisdiction — a hard no under the firm's GDPR data processing agreements; (b) she needs an audit trail of every AI-generated output used in a client deliverable; (c) cost visibility — per-seat SaaS at 200 users is opaque and grows with headcount.

---

## Journey

**Trigger:** María receives a 180-page SPA at 09:00 for a cross-border acquisition closing in 72 hours.

**Step 1 — Document ingestion (Carlos, pre-session)**  
Legal ops has already ingested the firm's historical SPA corpus (3,200 executed agreements) into Qdrant via the `haystack-mcp` pipeline. Each document is chunked at 512 tokens with 64-token overlap, embedded with `text-embedding-3-small` via OpenRouter, and stored in the `legal-corpus` collection. The `filesystem` MCP server gives LobeChat write access to a scoped `/data/matters/` directory. No document ever leaves the EC2.

**Step 2 — María opens LobeChat, loads the new SPA**  
She uploads the SPA PDF through the MinIO-backed file upload. LobeChat stores it in the `lobe` bucket on the local MinIO instance (port 47005). The model used at this step is `openrouter/claude-sonnet` or `openrouter/llama-3.3-70b` — the firm is not locked to any provider.

**Step 3 — Clause extraction (MCP: filesystem + qdrant-mcp)**  
María types: *"Extract all limitation of liability clauses from this SPA and compare each against our standard positions."*

LobeChat routes this to the active agent. The model calls:
1. `filesystem-read_file` → reads the uploaded SPA from `/data/matters/matter-2024-089/spa-draft.pdf`
2. `qdrant-mcp` → `qdrant-find` against `legal-corpus` with query `"limitation of liability SPA standard positions"` → returns top-8 chunks from historical SPAs with cosine similarity > 0.82

The model synthesises: here are the 3 LoL clauses in the draft, here is what the firm has accepted/rejected historically, here are the 2 deviations that require partner review. Output rendered as a `lobe-artifacts` markdown table inside chat.

**Step 4 — Redline drafting (MCP: filesystem)**  
María asks: *"Draft suggested redline language for clause 12.3 based on the positions we have successfully negotiated in the last 18 months."*

The model calls `qdrant-find` again with a time-filtered query (metadata field `executed_date > 2023-01-01`), retrieves 4 relevant precedents, and drafts replacement language. María reviews, edits inline, and saves via `filesystem-write_file` to `/data/matters/matter-2024-089/redline-v1.md`.

**Step 5 — Audit log (automatic)**  
Every message, tool call, and model response is stored in Postgres (`messages`, `message_plugins` tables). Carlos can pull a complete audit trail of what the AI suggested and what María kept. This is the evidence trail the insurer requires.

**Step 6 — Partner escalation**  
Elena receives a Slack notification (via `linux-sandbox` MCP running a curl webhook) with a summary of the flagged deviations. She approves or requests further review directly in LobeChat via a shared session link.

## Why this stack vs ChatGPT/Copilot

**Reason 1 — Data never leaves the firm's infrastructure**  
ChatGPT Enterprise and Copilot both route data through Microsoft/OpenAI US datacentres. Under the firm's GDPR data processing agreements with clients, transferring client-confidential deal documents to a third-party processor outside the EU requires explicit DPA amendments — a 6–8 week legal process per client, per matter. With the self-hosted stack on an EC2 in `eu-west-1`, all data stays within EU jurisdiction, within the firm's own AWS account, encrypted at rest (EBS gp3 with AES-256). There is no sub-processor disclosure obligation because there is no sub-processor.

**Reason 2 — MCP tools create a proprietary knowledge loop that SaaS cannot replicate**  
ChatGPT Enterprise can search the public web. It cannot search the firm's 15 years of executed SPAs stored in their DMS, because those documents are not and will never be uploaded to OpenAI's infrastructure. The `qdrant-mcp` + `haystack-mcp` stack creates a retrieval layer over the firm's own corpus — a durable competitive advantage that compounds as more matters are indexed. Microsoft Copilot for M365 can search SharePoint, but it cannot run a structured semantic search against a curated vector corpus with metadata filtering (practice area, counterparty jurisdiction, deal size). The gap is not the chat interface — it is the retrieval architecture.

**Reason 3 — Cost structure is predictable and decoupled from headcount**  
ChatGPT Enterprise is priced at approximately $60/user/month. At 200 users that is $144,000/year, growing linearly with every new hire and with no volume discount below 1,000 seats. The self-hosted stack runs on a single `c7a.2xlarge` in dev/stage and an `m7a.4xlarge` in prod — fixed infrastructure cost of roughly $400–600/month regardless of whether 20 or 200 people use it that day. Token cost is variable but capped by the firm's OpenRouter budget, not by per-seat pricing. As the firm grows from 200 to 400 lawyers, the marginal cost of the AI platform is near zero — a fundamentally different economic model.

**Reason 4 (bonus) — Model flexibility without re-procurement**  
When a better open-weight model ships — e.g. a fine-tuned legal LLM — the firm can swap it in by changing one environment variable (`OPENROUTER_DEFAULT_MODEL` or pointing vLLM at a new HuggingFace checkpoint). With ChatGPT Enterprise or Copilot, the model is whatever Microsoft or OpenAI decides to ship next quarter. For a firm where the quality of legal reasoning in AI outputs is a professional liability question, control over the model is not a nice-to-have.
